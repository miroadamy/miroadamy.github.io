Napoli, Three Thousand Years Deep

I spent a few days in Napoli last week, with a day in Pompeii. It was not my first visit, so I have two data points now, and the second one confirmed the first: it is a fascinating city, and it is nothing like the postcard.

The city
#

Napoli is not clean. It is not well maintained. Its aesthetics are strange, a baroque palazzo with laundry hanging from the piano nobile, a scooter parked inside the church gate, a street market under a seventeenth-century arch. And it is very much alive, in a way that few cities of its size still are. The life happens on the street, loudly, and nobody seems to be performing it for the visitors.

The Quartieri Spagnoli on a weekday evening. The banner says “welcome, get lost in these magnificent alleys”, and people do.

The Quartieri Spagnoli are the concentrated version of that: a grid of narrow streets laid out in the sixteenth century for the Spanish garrison, climbing steeply from Via Toledo, every balcony in a different colour and most of them occupied. It is also where the hill starts. Napoli is a hilly city in a way maps do not show. The historic centre sits on a slope that runs from the port up to the Vomero, and the way the city solved that is my favourite piece of its infrastructure: the funiculars. Four of them, part of the regular transport network, same ticket as the metro, and full of people going to work, not tourists. Riding one up to the Vomero and walking back down through the alleys is the best half-day I can recommend.

The walk down from the Vomero. Where the hill is too steep for a street, the street becomes a staircase, and the sea is at the bottom of every one of them.

The same slope seen sideways: palazzi, terraces and gardens stacked on top of each other, and the bay behind.

And there is the mountain. The special feeling of Napoli, the one I cannot shake, is living in the shadow of a volcano that obliterated two great cities, Herculaneum and Pompeii, not so long ago in geological terms. Nineteen and a half centuries is nothing for a volcano. Vesuvius is in the view from almost everywhere, the bay curves around it, and three million people live in the area it last buried. The city does not seem to mind.

The bay from the Certosa di San Martino: the port, Castel Nuovo, and Vesuvius with its two summits across the water.

The crowds
#

The crowds are everywhere. A bit less in October than in summer, but still very noticeable, especially over the weekend when the centro storico fills up by late morning and stays full past midnight. Via dei Tribunali, the pizza streets, the Spaccanapoli: you do not walk, you flow.

A thought for anybody considering a trip to India or to one of Asia’s hugely populated cities: visit Napoli in summer first. It is a good litmus test of whether you want to go at all. If the density, the noise and the honking here get to you, the real thing will be a different order of magnitude. If you come out of it smiling, you are ready.

Pompeii
#

A few thoughts from Pompeii, the second visit there as well.

After more than two and a half centuries of digging, they still have not uncovered everything. About a third of the ancient city is still under the ash, and excavations are ongoing: the new finds in the papers every few months come from there. Walking the excavated two thirds already takes a full day and your legs, and the streets just keep going.

The Basilica, the law court and exchange next to the forum. The brick stumps were columns plastered to look like stone, which is a very Roman way of building a courthouse.

The size and sophistication of Roman society is what stays with me. Not the villas with the frescoes, impressive as they are, but the ordinary infrastructure: paved streets with stepping stones for the pedestrians and ruts worn by the carts, water fountains at the corners fed by lead pipes, the fast-food counters with the pots set into the marble, the bakeries with their mills, the graffiti about elections and gladiators. A provincial town of maybe twelve thousand people had all of that, in the first century. We like to think of the past as primitive. Pompeii argues otherwise.

A peristyle garden in one of the big houses: frescoed walls, marble basins, a colonnade around a garden replanted the way the root cavities showed it. Somebody lived like this in a provincial town.

And then the casts. In the nineteenth century the excavators noticed hollows in the hardened ash and poured plaster into them, and out came the shapes of the people the ash had trapped: a man covering his face, a family in a row, a dog. They are not sculptures, although they look like them, and that is what makes them heartbreaking. The hollow is the exact shape of a person at the moment of dying, with the folds of the clothes, in a glass case, almost two thousand years later.

Depth of time
#

A thought that started on the funicular and that I am still turning over: the cities I have lived in, or spent real time in, can be ordered by their depth of time. How far back does the ground under your feet remember?

By far the youngest is Ottawa, where I spent almost two decades. In the early 1800s there was nothing there but forest and a river; the town was founded in 1826 as a construction camp for the Rideau Canal, and it became Ottawa in 1855. Everything you see is younger than the oldest buildings on my street in Bratislava.

Bratislava, where I live now (again), goes back about a thousand years. The first written mention is from 907, a battle near a castle called Brezalauspurc, at least as the current understanding of history says. The castle hill was settled long before, but a thousand years is roughly what the city itself can show you.

When we moved to Valencia, we gained another thousand years. The city was founded by the Romans in 138 BC as Valentia, and you can feel it and see it: the forum is under the cathedral square, the Roman street grid is still the street grid, and the excavations under the Almoina show the baths, the forum and the Visigothic church, one on top of the other.

And Napoli is a different league. Another almost thousand years: Greek settlers from Cumae founded Parthenope on the Pizzofalcone hill in the 8th century BC, and in the 6th century BC the new city, Neapolis, went up next to it. The name is still Greek, the three main streets of the old centre are still the Greek decumani, and the Greek walls come up to the surface in Piazza Bellini. You walk down Spaccanapoli on a street that has been a street for twenty-five centuries.

So the ladder goes 200, 1,000, 2,000, 3,000 years. Each step down the ladder was a move south and east, and each one made the previous city feel younger than I had thought. I do not know what the next step would be, although right now I am not planning to find out, Bratislava is deep enough for one lifetime ;-)
